## The question

Political imagery surrounds us daily, in the news, on social media and in campaigns, shaping how we perceive reality. We usually ask what people *think* about these images.

I wanted to ask a different question: how do we *look* at them? Our eyes choose where to land, what to linger on and what to avoid, before we've decided what we think.

## How it works

With a laptop webcam and real-time facial-landmark detection, the system calibrates to each viewer's gaze, then tracks where they look while viewing a set of political photographs, measuring fixations, saccades and attention density.

## Key decisions and trade-offs

Every design decision here was also a research decision.

<Decisions>
  <Decision
    title="A webcam, not an eye tracker"
    choice="Run entirely in the browser with a laptop webcam and MediaPipe facial landmarks. No extra hardware."

## Solution

### Calibration

Before data collection, the viewer completes a 9-point calibration, holding their gaze on each point while the system builds a personal mapping between iris position and screen coordinates.

  src="250ms/calibration"

### The viewing session

The session records fixations, scanpaths, first glance and attention density. Age, gender and location are logged as well, to allow comparison across viewers.

### The political arc

Each region the gaze lingers on is mapped to a rough position on a left-right arc, based on who occupies that region.

## Design system

The sky is where the eye rests between charged images. Clouds ask nothing of you and take no side. That innocence is the point, because looking at a political image is never neutral.

Over that calm backdrop sits a thin red grid and deep red typography, the visual language of surveillance and lab instruments.

## Iteration

Webcam tracking is noisy. Even a steady, fixated gaze visibly trembled on screen.

More smoothing everywhere would have made real eye movements feel sluggish, so I built an adaptive filter that detects when the eye is actually still and smooths hard only then. It cut visible tremor by over 50%, with no added lag during real movement. [TODO: how the 50% was measured]
