## The question

Political imagery surrounds us daily, in the news, on social media and in campaigns, shaping how we perceive reality. We usually ask what people *think* about these images. I wanted to ask a different question: how do we *look* at them? Our eyes choose where to land, what to linger on and what to avoid.

## How it works – concept overview

With nothing but a laptop webcam and real-time facial-landmark detection, the system calibrates to each viewer's gaze, then tracks exactly where they look while viewing a set of political photographs, measuring fixations, saccades and attention density down to the pixel.

## Process – research & approach

Every design decision here was a research decision: how many images balance statistical value against fatigue? How long should each image stay before attention drifts toward boredom rather than genuine interest?

The system settled on 16 images at 3 seconds each: long enough for a first fixation and a scan, short enough to stay honest.

Each region a viewer's gaze lingers on is mapped to a rough position on a left-right political arc, based on who occupies that region. This is deliberately presented as an artistic, conceptual interpretation, not a scientific measurement, and that framing is stated on screen.

## Design system

The sky is where the eye rests between charged images. Clouds ask nothing of you, take no side and provoke no feeling. That innocence is the point: it is the exact opposite of looking at a political image, which is never neutral. Over that calm backdrop sits a thin red grid and deep red typography, the visual language of surveillance and lab instruments.

## Challenges & iteration

Webcam tracking is noisy by nature. One challenge: a steady, fixated gaze still visibly trembled on screen. The fix wasn't more smoothing everywhere, which would have made real eye movements feel sluggish, but an adaptive filter that detects when the eye is actually still and smooths hard only then. It cut visible tremor by over 50%, with no added lag during real movement. [TODO: how the 50% was measured]
